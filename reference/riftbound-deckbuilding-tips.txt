RIFTBOUND TCG - DECKBUILDING TIPS COMPILED FROM ONLINE SOURCES
With the reasoning behind each tip
===============================================================================

Compiled: July 29, 2026
Current set: Unleashed (Set 3). Vendetta (Set 4) releases July 31, 2026.

ABOUT THIS DOCUMENT
-------------------
This gathers deckbuilding advice from eight sources, including Riot's own
official primer written by Game Director Dave Guskin. Every tip is paired with
the stated or implied reasoning, because a rule of thumb you don't understand
is a rule of thumb you'll misapply.

Where sources disagree or where advice has gone stale, that is flagged
explicitly rather than smoothed over. See Section 11.

Full source list at the end (Section 13).


===============================================================================
SECTION 1 - THE FIXED NUMBERS
===============================================================================

Riftbound locks most of its deck sizes. Learn these first; the strategy only
matters once the deck is legal.

  Main Deck ............ exactly 40 cards (Constructed format)
  Rune Deck ............ exactly 12 cards
  Legend ............... 1 card (Legend Zone, outside both decks)
  Chosen Champion ...... 1 card (Champion Zone, outside both decks)
  Battlefields ......... 3 unique cards, selected pre-game
  Sideboard ............ 0 or up to 10 cards (see Section 9 - this changed
                         on July 24, 2026, and most guides online still
                         say 8)

Total physical cards you bring: 57, plus sideboard.

A nuance worth knowing: the general rules permit a "regular" deck of at least
40 main deck cards, but the Constructed format narrows that to exactly 40.
Riot's primer notes you technically can go above 40 and advises against it.

  WHY 40 AND NOT MORE: Every card above 40 dilutes your access to your best
  cards - specifically your Champion copies and Signature cards. A bigger deck
  is a worse deck because it draws its good cards less often. This is the
  single most common instinct new players have to unlearn.

  WHY THE CHAMPION SLOT MATTERS: One copy of your Chosen Champion starts on
  the table in the Champion Zone, and that copy counts against your 3-copy
  limit. So the Champion is effectively a card you are guaranteed to have
  every single game. Build around it accordingly.


===============================================================================
SECTION 2 - STEP ONE: CHOOSE THE LEGEND
===============================================================================

Every source agrees the Legend is the first decision, not a later one.

  WHY IT'S FIRST: The Legend does three things simultaneously - it sets your
  Domain Identity (the only two rune colors you may ever use), it dictates
  which Champion and Signature cards are legal for you, and it grants an
  always-on Legend Ability. Changing your Legend later means rebuilding the
  deck from scratch, which in a paper TCG means rebuying it too.

THE SIX DOMAINS AND WHAT EACH IS GOOD AND BAD AT
------------------------------------------------
Riot's primer is unusually candid about each domain's designed weaknesses.
This is the most useful section of the official guide, because knowing what a
domain CAN'T do tells you what your deck will need to cover.

FURY (Red) - aggression, passion, conquering
  Strong at: damage-based removal, Accelerate and other ways to enter ready,
             payoffs for conquering
  Weak at:   drawing lots of cards; killing units directly, especially units
             sitting safely in their base out of combat

CALM (Green) - balance, meditation, holding
  Strong at: defensive combat tricks, moving enemy units, negating
             interaction (counterspell and stun effects)
  Weak at:   being genuinely aggressive; interacting without using its units

MIND (Blue) - innovation, outsmarting, curiosity
  Strong at: long-term planning, shrinking effects, card draw, hidden and
             gear synergies
  Weak at:   moving units; raw unit efficiency

BODY (Orange) - physical superiority, agility, the wilds
  Strong at: resource ramp, efficient high-impact units, winning combats
  Weak at:   efficient card draw; non-combat spells

CHAOS (Purple) - unpredictability, rebellion, trickiness
  Strong at: position and trash manipulation, hidden tricks, card filtering
  Weak at:   permanently removing a threat; granting permanent buffs

ORDER (Yellow) - unity, community, sacrifice
  Strong at: generating many small units, death triggers, killing units
             directly
  Weak at:   handling small units; moving units

  HOW TO USE THIS: Your two domains will share some blind spots. A Calm/Mind
  deck, for example, is doubly weak at aggression and at unit efficiency - so
  it needs a game plan that wins slowly and a reason its cheap units are
  acceptable. A Fury/Body deck has almost no card draw between its two
  domains, so it must win before running out of gas. Identify the overlapping
  weakness early; it defines your deck's real losing condition.

TIP: Pick a Legend whose ability matches how you actually enjoy playing.
  WHY: Multiple sources make the point that most Legends are viable when built
  correctly, so the limiting factor is usually pilot familiarity rather than
  raw power. A Legend you understand deeply beats a stronger Legend you don't.

TIP: Read the Legend Ability as a statement of archetype.
  WHY: The ability usually telegraphs the intended plan. Token generation
  points toward swarm and aggro. Rune channeling points toward ramp and a
  late-game plan. Card draw on an empty hand points toward emptying your hand
  fast. Cheap recurring buffs point toward either going wide or building one
  enormous threat, depending on which Champion you pair with it.

TIP: But don't treat the telegraphed archetype as mandatory.
  WHY: Sources note that Legends with the same domain pair can support
  genuinely different strategies, and that creative builds are rewarded when
  fundamentals are solid. Two Legends sharing a domain pair and even a similar
  ability can want different curves - a buff aimed at a lone defender implies
  a defensive midrange deck, while a smaller buff usable on anything leaves
  the aggressive door open.


===============================================================================
SECTION 3 - STEP TWO: CHOOSE THE CHOSEN CHAMPION
===============================================================================

Each Legend has multiple Champion Unit versions, typically one per domain.
Exactly one goes in the Champion Zone.

TIP: Choose the Champion whose ability matches the deck shape you want, then
build toward it - not the one with the biggest numbers.
  WHY: Riot's primer illustrates this with two versions of the same champion.
  One version boosts other already-buffed units, which pushes you toward a
  wide board of many units that buff themselves. The other version can stack
  unlimited buffs on itself, which pushes you toward one enormous threat and
  concentrating every buff on it. Same Legend, same ability, opposite
  decklists. The Chosen Champion is the fork in the road.

TIP: Run extra copies of your Chosen Champion in the main deck.
  WHY: You built the strategy around having access to it, and opponents will
  remove it. Extra copies are insurance on your own game plan. Remember the
  table copy counts toward the 3 maximum.

TIP: You may also run other Champion Units, including ones with different
tags, as long as they fit your Domain Identity.
  WHY: This is a common misreading of the rules. Only the CHOSEN Champion
  must share a tag with your Legend. Your main deck can include up to 3 copies
  of any Champion Unit that is color-legal, and you can mix multiple Champion
  Units that share your tag to blend strategies.

TIP: Know the turn your Champion is supposed to arrive, and don't just deploy
it as soon as you can afford it.
  WHY: Because the Chosen Champion is always available, the decision is
  purely about timing rather than drawing it. Guides identify "comes down at
  the right moment" as a property of a good Champion alongside curve fit and
  immediate board impact. Deploying it into removal on turn three when it
  wins the game on turn six is a real and common way to lose.


===============================================================================
SECTION 4 - STEP THREE: SIGNATURE CARDS
===============================================================================

Signature cards are Spells or Units tied to a Champion Tag. Limit: 3 total per
matching Champion Tag. They can never go in the Champion Zone.

TIP: Default to the maximum 3 copies of your Signature card, and require a
specific reason to run fewer.
  WHY: Riot's primer states plainly that Signature cards are more powerful
  than other cards of comparable cost, and that they're designed to plug into
  that champion's specific strategy. They are among the highest-value slots in
  the deck. If your list doesn't want them, that's usually a signal your build
  has drifted away from the Legend's plan.

TIP: Count your Signature cards toward your interactive-spell quota.
  WHY: Many Signature spells already do the swing-a-combat job described in
  Section 5. If yours does, you need fewer other interactive spells - don't
  double-count the requirement and end up spell-heavy.

WATCH OUT: A Signature card with the wrong tag, or a 4th copy, makes the deck
illegal. So does putting a Signature Unit in the Champion Zone.

SECTION 5 - STEP FOUR: FILLING THE MAIN DECK
===============================================================================

This is where the concrete numeric guidance lives. Sources broadly converge.

TIP: Play at least 9 cheap units, roughly in the 2-4 cost range.
  WHY (Riot's stated reason): You cannot win without fighting for battlefield
  control, and you need enough cheap bodies that you reliably draw one by your
  first or second turn. Riftbound.gg gives a similar figure at 9-12 low-cost
  units. The exception Riot names: if your Legend Ability itself helps you
  contest battlefields early, you can afford fewer.

TIP: Play at least 6 interactive spells.
  WHY: Showdowns in Riftbound are frequently decided by a single card. An
  interactive spell is anything that improves your unit, weakens theirs, or
  removes theirs outright. Six-plus is the threshold Riot gives for reliably
  having drawn one by mid-game. Riftbound.gg suggests 6-9 low-to-medium cost
  spells with a couple of expensive powerful ones on top.

TIP: When in doubt, cut a spell and add a unit.
  WHY: Every source repeats this. Units are what physically hold battlefields
  and therefore what scores points. Spells and gear support board presence;
  they don't create it. It is very easy to fall in love with a pile of clever
  spells and build a deck that cannot actually stand on a battlefield.

TIP: Don't load up on expensive finishers.
  WHY: The win condition in Riftbound is claiming and holding battlefields,
  which is a board-presence problem, not a haymaker problem. A deck stuffed
  with high-cost closers loses the battlefields before the closers arrive.

TIP: Have a specific, named win condition and confirm you can actually
execute it.
  WHY: Mobalytics frames the requirement as being able to either hold a
  battlefield or score several on one turn. Riot frames it the same way. In
  practice that means either substantial defensive units and protective
  spells, or an aggressive force you can assemble in the late game. Both are
  valid; having neither is not. Check for this explicitly at the end of
  building, because it is easy to assemble 40 reasonable cards with no plan.

TIP: Include some card draw, whatever your colors.
  WHY: Mobalytics notes some domains have far better access than others, but
  advises every deck include something. Cross-reference the domain weaknesses
  in Section 2 - if both your domains are listed as poor at card draw, this
  becomes a real constraint on how long a game you can afford to play.

TIP: Include ways to come back from behind, or to redeploy a stuck army.
  WHY: Riot's worked example calls this out as addressing a specific
  archetype weakness. A slow build-up deck struggles against faster decks and
  against wider decks. Cards that let units enter ready immediately let you
  pressure a battlefield from a losing position, and let you move on from an
  army that is pinned defending one location when the points are elsewhere.
  Identify your deck's characteristic way of losing, then dedicate slots to it.

A CAUTION ON RATIO FRAMEWORKS
-----------------------------
One competitive guide (Card Brawlers) proposes a distribution of 20-24 core
strategy cards, 12-16 support cards, 8-12 disruption cards, and 2-4 tech
cards, described as derived from tournament results.

Treat this as a way of thinking, not arithmetic. The minimums alone sum to 42,
which already exceeds the legal 40-card main deck, so the categories must be
overlapping rather than exclusive. The useful takeaway is the proportions:
roughly half your deck advancing your plan, a quarter enabling it, a quarter
interacting with the opponent, and a small number of meta-specific answers.

SECTION 6 - STEP FIVE: THE RUNE DECK
===============================================================================

Twelve runes, split between your two domains in any ratio you like. This is
the part sources warn about most emphatically.

FIRST, THE MECHANIC THAT DRIVES EVERYTHING
------------------------------------------
You channel 2 runes per turn. Each rune can be exhausted for Energy or
recycled for Power. Energy is generic - any rune produces it. Power is
color-specific - to pay a Fury Power cost you must recycle a Fury rune. There
are no substitutes.

  THEREFORE: Your rune ratio needs to serve your POWER costs specifically,
  not your general color balance. A deck can be half Mind cards by count and
  still want only four Mind runes, if none of those Mind cards demand Mind
  Power.

TIP: Start at 6/6, then adjust based on play experience.
  WHY: It's the neutral default and the recommendation in Riot's primer, the
  Riftbound wiki, and riftbound.gg. It works when Power costs are genuinely
  balanced across both domains. It is more punishing than a skewed base when
  your channel draws come in the wrong order.

TIP: Count your actual Power costs by color, then skew the base to match.
  WHY: This is the concrete method every guide converges on. Go through all
  40 cards, tally which color each Power symbol demands, and tilt the 12
  accordingly. Runes & Rift gives the example that roughly 15 cards demanding
  Fury Power justifies 7 or 8 Fury runes. Standard skews are 7/5 and 8/4.
  Too few of a color produces unplayable hands and wasted turns.

TIP: A deliberate design pattern - put your zero-Power cards in one domain
and your Power-hungry cards in the other, then skew hard, around 8/4.
  WHY: This is Riot's own suggestion and it is more actionable than generic
  balance advice. If one domain's contribution to your deck costs no Power at
  all, those cards are castable off any rune, and you can commit almost your
  whole rune base to the domain that actually needs specific colors. You get
  consistency in both directions instead of hedging.

TIP: Don't skew so hard that your secondary color becomes unfindable.
  WHY: Danireon raises a point the other guides miss - the ratio also
  constrains your channeling CHOICES each turn. At a 10/2 split you will
  almost always channel the dominant color, which is reliable, but it leaves
  you helpless in the games where you need the secondary domain and simply
  cannot find it in the deck.

TIP: Balance Power-hungry cards with efficient no-Power cards.
  WHY: The strongest cards often carry Power costs, but a deck full of them
  stalls. Runes & Rift's framing: the more zero-Power cards you include, the
  smoother the curve feels across the whole game.

TIP: Plan recycling a turn ahead.
  WHY: Rift Mana flags a consequence that isn't obvious from the card text -
  recycling reduces your total rune count, so a big Power turn leaves you with
  fewer resources the following turn. Before committing to a Power-heavy play,
  ask what next turn looks like.

TIP: Consider Seal cards for a domain you're heavily invested in.
  WHY: Riot's primer notes Seals help pay a domain's recycle costs repeatedly
  over multiple turns, so they scale with how many cards in that domain demand
  Power. The more concentrated your Power needs, the better Seals get.

SECTION 7 - STEP SIX: BATTLEFIELDS
===============================================================================

Three unique battlefields, chosen before the game. Only one is live per game.
In best-of-one it's randomly selected from your three; in best-of-three you
actively choose, and no battlefield may be used twice in a match.

RULES DETAIL (verified against Core Rules RUP4, 2026-07-16, 2026-08-05):
  The two sanctioned 1v1 modes differ here and the difference is load-bearing.
    - Duel (485), Bo1: each player selects one of their three AT RANDOM
      (485.5). The other two are removed.
    - Match (486), Bo3: each player CHOOSES one (486.5), placed
      simultaneously. Battlefields used in a game someone WON are removed for
      the rest of the match. If a game had no winner, they may be re-used
      (486.5.a). In the Bo5 variant a battlefield may be presented a second
      time once all three have been presented, never a third (486.6.a).

  THE BATTLEFIELD IS COMMITTED BEFORE TURN ORDER AND BEFORE YOUR HAND. Setup
  order is 110-118: legend (111), chosen champion (112), battlefields per mode
  (113), shuffle (114), TURN ORDER by fair random method (115), draw 4 (116),
  mulligan up to 2 (117), begin (118). So game 1's presentation is blind to
  who is on the play, and can never be tuned to a keepable opening hand.

  The only mechanical asymmetry between the seats: the player going second
  channels an extra rune on their first Channel Phase (485.7, 486.7). Going
  first buys tempo; going second buys a rune of slack.

  NOT IN THE RULES, though widely repeated - treat as table/tournament
  convention and confirm against the event document:
    - "placed face down, then revealed simultaneously." The rules say only
      "placed simultaneously" (485.5, 486.5).
    - "the loser of the previous game decides who goes first." Rule 115 says
      fair random method, and 486 adds no game-2 exception. If this convention
      does NOT apply, every game is blind to turn order and a neutral pick is
      worth more than a seat-specific one.

TIP: Give each of the three a ROLE, don't make three independent picks.
  WHY: In Match play you choose which to present each game, so the set should
  cover the three situations you can be in.
    1. GO-FIRST / proactive - rewards already having a body down and
       contesting early; converts a tempo lead into points. Allowed to be bad
       from behind, because you only present it knowing you're on the play.
    2. GO-SECOND / reactive - stabilises, accelerates resources, or rewards
       catching up; pairs with the extra rune the second player channels.
       This is the one that must be good from behind.
    3. NEUTRAL - the game-1 presentation, made blind. Leans on your deck's
       structural synergy, gives a generic opposing deck as little as
       possible, and doesn't care who is on the play. "Blank for them,
       quietly good for me" beats "very strong for whoever is ahead."
  Sequence in a Bo3: neutral in game 1, then the seat-matching one in games
  2-3 - assuming the loser-chooses convention above actually applies.

TIP: Ask three questions, in this order (Riot's framework):
  1. Which battlefields most support my strategy?
  2. Which would be disastrous for me if my OPPONENT controlled them?
  3. Which are slightly better for me than for my opponent?

  WHY THE SECOND QUESTION MATTERS MOST: Battlefield effects apply to BOTH
  players. This is the mistake to avoid. A battlefield that supercharges your
  plan may supercharge your opponent's more. Runes & Rift puts it well - pick
  the ones you will use better than your opponent, not the ones you like.

TIP: Factor in turn order.
  WHY: Rift Mana raises a consideration absent from the beginner guides -
  some battlefields are substantially stronger for the player going first,
  while others are turn-order neutral. Since you don't control who goes first,
  a spread that includes neutral options is more robust than three
  first-player-dependent picks.

TIP: Use battlefields to patch your deck's holes, not just amplify strengths.
  WHY: Runes & Rift specifically suggests covering weak spots - a battlefield
  granting draw or extra rune channeling can substitute for deck slots you
  couldn't spare. Cross-reference Section 2: if both your domains are bad at
  card draw, a draw battlefield is doing real work.

TIP: In best-of-three, plan the SET of three, not three independent picks.
  WHY: Because you must win with two different battlefields to win a match,
  your three picks need to cover different game shapes. Riftbound.gg frames
  this as deciding which is best for games one and two and which is best for
  the tiebreaker. A trio of near-identical battlefields wastes the format.

TIP: Battlefields are colorless and unrestricted.
  WHY: Origins alone offers 20 colorless battlefields usable by any deck.
  There is no Domain Identity restriction here, so this is the one part of
  deckbuilding where the entire pool is open to you. Use that.

SECTION 8 - CONSISTENCY AND THE THREE-COPY LIMIT
===============================================================================

Maximum 3 copies of any card by full name.

TIP: Run as many cards at 3 copies as is reasonable.
  WHY: Consistency wins card games. Runes & Rift's illustration: 13 different
  cards at 3 copies each (39 cards) plus one Champion is an extremely
  consistent deck - it mulligans well, curves smoothly, and repeats its good
  early games. The more singletons you run, the more your deck plays
  differently every time you shuffle.

TIP: But audit your copy distribution before finalising.
  WHY: Danireon raises the counterweight - a deck maxed out on everything is
  one-dimensional and can fold entirely to a single counter-strategy. Some
  archetypes want a wider spread of unique names for matchup flexibility.
  Count how many cards sit at 3, how many at 1 or 2, and make that
  distribution a deliberate choice rather than an accident.

TIP: A few 1-of tech cards are fine; a deck built from 1-ofs is not.
  WHY: The surprise value of a singleton is real but small, and it is paid for
  with consistency across every game you play. The 40 slots are fixed - how
  you distribute them is where deckbuilding actually happens.

SECTION 9 - THE SIDEBOARD
===============================================================================

IMPORTANT CURRENCY NOTE: The sideboard maximum increased from 8 to 10 cards
effective July 24, 2026. Most guides online, including riftbound.gg's
otherwise excellent Deckbuilding 101, still state "exactly 8 or 0." Main deck,
rune, battlefield, Domain Identity, and copy-limit rules all still apply to
sideboard cards.

TIP: Copy limits span deck AND sideboard combined.
  WHY: This catches people at deck checks. If you run 3 copies of a card in
  the main deck, you cannot add a 4th to the sideboard. Two main plus one
  sideboard is legal.

TIP: Sideboard against the specific decks you expect to face.
  WHY: Riftbound.gg's advice is to target the top-tier Legends you'll actually
  sit across from. Generic "good cards" in a sideboard are wasted slots -
  their job is to convert bad matchups, and a card that's mildly useful
  everywhere converts nothing.

TIP: Battlefields are legal sideboard cards.
  *** WRONG -- CORRECTED 2026-09-25. Battlefields may NOT be sideboarded. ***
  Tournament Rules 601.1.c.2: "A sideboard can consist only of valid Main Deck
  cards." A battlefield is not a Main Deck card -- each player provides exactly
  3 separately (601.1.a). And 403.4.b is explicit: "In constructed formats, a
  player may not change their Runes, Legend, or Battlefields at any point after
  deck registration." Flagged by the project owner, confirmed against
  data/tournament_rules.txt.

TIP: Run zero sideboard cards if you only play best-of-one.
  *** STALE -- CORRECTED 2026-09-25. Bo1 sideboarding IS permitted, at the
  head judge's option. *** Tournament Rules 406.1.g lets the head judge use an
  altered Bo1 setup "to closer mirror the best of 3 experience", and 406.1.g.5
  gives players "the opportunity to sideboard as if they were in between games
  of a best of 3 match". So it is event-dependent rather than forbidden, and
  recently it has typically been allowed (project owner). 0 is still a legal
  sideboard size, but not for this reason.

TIP: Situational-but-powerful cards belong here, not in the main deck.
  WHY: Cards with a fantastic power-to-cost ratio against one narrow target
  are dead draws in most matchups. That profile is precisely what a sideboard
  slot is for. Vendetta's Decree cycle - one spell per domain, each especially
  effective against its opposite domain - is a clean example of the pattern.

SECTION 10 - THE LEGALITY CHECKLIST
===============================================================================

Run this before any event. Each of these makes a deck illegal, not merely bad.

  [ ] Main deck contains exactly 40 cards
  [ ] Rune deck contains exactly 12 runes
  [ ] Sideboard contains 0 cards, or no more than 10
  [ ] 1 Legend in the Legend Zone
  [ ] 1 Champion Unit in the Champion Zone, sharing a tag with the Legend
  [ ] 3 unique battlefields selected
  [ ] Every card matches the Legend's Domain Identity
  [ ] For dual-symbol cards, BOTH symbols fall within your identity
  [ ] Every rune matches one of your two domains
  [ ] No more than 3 copies of any card name, counting main deck + sideboard,
      and counting the Champion Zone copy
  [ ] No more than 3 Signature cards per matching Champion Tag
  [ ] No Signature Unit sitting in the Champion Zone
  [ ] No cards currently on the Standard ban list

  ON THE OFF-COLOR RULE: A single off-color card makes the whole deck illegal.
  Sources identify this as the mistake that catches new players most often,
  particularly on dual-symbol cards where people check only one symbol.

  ON BANS: [CORRECTED 2026-08-05 against Riot's official Banned Cards page.
  This passage previously listed only three bans -- Stealthy Pursuer, The
  Arena's Greatest, Aspirant's Climb -- and was badly incomplete.]

  As of July 24, 2026 the Constructed ban list is TEN cards, and Riot bans
  outright rather than restricting (there is no restricted list):

    Battlefields (5): Aspirant's Climb, Obelisk of Power, Reaver's Row,
                      The Arena's Greatest, The Dreaming Tree
    Gear (1):         Scrapheap
    Spells (2):       Called Shot, Fight or Flight
    Units (2):        Draven - Vanquisher, Stealthy Pursuer

  2v2 Constructed additionally bans one Legend of its own: the Starter
  printing of Master Yi - Wuju Bladesman (for overshadowing every other 2v2
  Legend option). That one is legal in 1v1.

  HALF THE LIST IS BATTLEFIELDS. Battlefields are the line nobody re-reads
  when copying a decklist, so check bans BEFORE you rate battlefields, not
  after. Machine-readable copy: data/banlist.json, enforced by
  `python3 cli.py check`. Re-scrape Riot's Banned Cards tab when a new wave
  lands -- nothing is hardcoded, the checker reads that file.

SECTION 11 - WHERE SOURCES DISAGREE, AND WHAT'S GONE STALE
===============================================================================

Being straight about this is more useful than presenting a false consensus.

1. "EXACTLY 40" VS "AT LEAST 40"
   Runes & Rift says exactly 40, no exceptions. Riot's primer says you can go
   higher but recommends against it. The League of Legends wiki resolves it:
   a "regular" deck requires at least 40 main deck cards, while the
   Constructed format specifically requires exactly 40. Riot's primer predates
   the Origins release. For any tournament play, treat it as exactly 40.

2. SIDEBOARD SIZE
   Guides say 8 or 0. The correct current figure is up to 10, changed July 24,
   2026. Anything written before that date is wrong on this point.

3. RUNE RATIO STARTING POINT
   Riot, the wiki, and riftbound.gg all say start at 6/6 and adjust. Runes &
   Rift and Danireon argue most competitive decks should lean 7/5 or 8/4 from
   the outset, treating 6/6 as the exception for genuinely balanced Power
   costs. These aren't really in conflict - 6/6 is the pedagogical default,
   the skew is the practical outcome once you count your Power symbols. Count
   first and you'll usually land on a skew.

4. THE COMPETITIVE RATIO FRAMEWORK
   The 20-24 / 12-16 / 8-12 / 2-4 breakdown from Card Brawlers cannot be read
   literally, since the minimums exceed 40 cards. Use the proportions, not the
   numbers.

5. TIER LISTS DISAGREE WITH EACH OTHER
   Different trackers rank the same Legends differently depending on whether
   they weight metashare, raw win rate, top-cut conversion, or outright wins.
   As of late July 2026, one tracker had Master Yi at Tier 1 with the highest
   metashare and win rate while a tier list elsewhere put Irelia alone at the
   top and Master Yi a tier below. Neither is wrong; they're measuring
   different things. Check what a list is actually measuring before you let it
   pick your deck.

6. SET ROTATION IS OFTEN MISDESCRIBED
   Riftbound's first Standard rotation does not occur until Set 10 arrives at
   the start of 2028, at which point Sets 1-5 leave. Nothing legal today
   rotates before then. Don't let rotation anxiety influence a build in 2026.


===============================================================================
SECTION 12 - META-AWARE BUILDING (SNAPSHOT, LATE JULY 2026)
===============================================================================

Deckbuilding advice ages fast. This section will be the first part of this
document to go stale - Vendetta releases July 31, 2026 and will reset much of
it.

TIP: Build with the actual field in mind, weighted by metashare.
  WHY: Your sideboard and your tech slots are only as good as your prediction
  of what you'll face. As of late July 2026 the Unleashed field ran roughly:
  Master Yi Wuju Bladesman 11%, Irelia Blade Dancer 9%, Diana Scorn of the
  Moon 8%, LeBlanc Deceiver 6%, Azir Emperor of the Sands 4%, Vex Gloomist 4%.
  A card that beats the 11% deck is worth far more than one that beats the 1%
  deck.

TIP: Distinguish metashare from win rate from conversion rate.
  WHY: These measure different things and reward different decisions.
  Metashare tells you what to prepare for. Win rate tells you how a deck
  performs head-to-head. Top-cut conversion tells you whether a deck
  over-performs relative to how often it's played. A deck can be popular and
  mediocre, or rare and excellent.

TIP: Check the ban list and the release calendar before investing money.
  WHY: A new set arriving in days can invalidate a build, shift singles
  prices, and change what's worth sideboarding. Ban list updates can make
  cards in a copied decklist outright illegal.

TIP: Beware of tuning too hard for a metagame that's about to change.
  WHY: Narrow tech aimed at a specific top deck is a bet on that deck still
  being there. Immediately before a set release, that bet is bad.
